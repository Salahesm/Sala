Interactive Narrative · Creative Coding 2026

The Things We Didn't Notice

A postcard about attention, convenience, and the parts of a place we make time to encounter.

p5.js Human–AI Interaction Interactive Storytelling Arts of Noticing

Concept: a finished task, an unfinished encounter

My original postcard paired the instruction to use AI for everything with a concern about losing our own participation in thinking. For this interactive version, that concern becomes a small visit to a forest. The visitor has a familiar task: write a postcard. The question is how that postcard comes into being. Does the visitor explore the place, accept ready-made descriptions, or combine assistance with personal attention?

The motivation is to create a moment of reflection about convenience. A completed piece of writing can look like evidence of an experience, even when its author has engaged very little with the subject. Here, the visitor can finish the postcard by repeatedly delegating its lines. The system produces acceptable sentences, but they contain fewer particular details than the observations available through exploration. This difference is deliberately authored. It expresses the project's question rather than demonstrating that real AI always writes generally or that using it necessarily weakens thinking.

Process: making meaning through rules

The postcard's landscape becomes a scene drawn with p5.js shapes: mountains, trees, water, mushrooms, and birds. Warm paper colors connect the environment to the original postcard. Moving reflections and birds give the place a continuing rhythm. Four numbered locations offer small narrative fragments. Their order is flexible, so each visitor can assemble a different sequence of observations.

The central design decision is to connect every action to the final postcard. Exploring a location contributes a specific observation. Delegating contributes a generic sentence and turns that location's marker blue. Repeated delegation also gradually cools the sky's colors. Asking for a hint highlights an unexplored location without writing its sentence. This third option matters because it gives assistance a constructive role: the system can direct attention while leaving the encounter to the visitor.

Each location contributes only one line per visit. This rule makes a choice consequential, while the restart button makes it possible to compare alternatives. The final postcard preserves the order of contributions and identifies their sources. Finishing early leaves some details unwritten. There is no winning score. The ending asks the visitor to consider which words feel connected to their own participation.

Stumbling blocks and design responses

A central design challenge is avoiding an overly simple moral in which technology is always harmful and independent action is always good. The hint option responds to this problem. Another challenge is representing attention without pretending to measure it. Clicking a marker cannot establish curiosity, understanding, or learning. The interaction therefore uses exploration as a symbolic action and explicitly describes its visual changes as metaphors.

A further tension is between discovery and accessibility. Completely hidden targets could make the environment frustrating, especially on a small screen. Numbered markers and matching HTML buttons make the available actions visible and provide keyboard access. These are design trade-offs in the prototype, rather than claims about difficulties observed in a user study.

Breakthrough: the postcard remembers the visit

The key conceptual breakthrough is making the postcard the record of the interaction. Instead of displaying the same warning after every visit, the ending contains the visitor's actual sequence of choices. Someone who delegates everything receives a different text from someone who explores. A mixed approach produces a mixed postcard. The narrative becomes personal within a small, deliberately limited set of possibilities.

Connections to the readings

Smed, Suovuo, Skult, and Skult (2021, pp. 4–6) describe interactive storytelling through the relationships among designer, platform, storyworld, and interactor. Their distinction between a designed storyworld and the story instance experienced through participation directly informs this project. The code defines the available places and rules, while the visitor determines the sequence and source of the postcard's lines. The result is bounded participation, not unlimited authorship.

Tsing's (2015, chapter 1) arts of noticing offers a second connection. Her discussion attends to lives and relationships overlooked by dominant narratives of progress, including mushroom gathering in disturbed forests. This project adapts that invitation to attend to overlooked details. The small mushrooms are especially relevant: the fallen branch becomes a place around which life continues. Tsing's argument concerns ecological and social relationships; applying it to AI-mediated attention is my interpretive extension, not her claim about AI.

The procedural meaning lies in how the rules distribute opportunities to participate. A hint leaves a discovery open; delegation fills a place in the postcard; exploration selects a specific detail. The system makes those differences visible through its outputs. It invites visitors to ask what kind of assistance they want, and what parts of an encounter they want to keep doing themselves.

References

  • Smed, J., Suovuo, T., Skult, N., & Skult, P. (2021). Handbook on interactive storytelling. Wiley. Chapter 1, supplied course excerpt.
  • Tsing, A. L. (2015). The mushroom at the end of the world: On the possibility of life in capitalist ruins . Princeton University Press. Chapter 1: Arts of Noticing.
